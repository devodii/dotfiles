The Visual Skeleton (The Layout)
The most important rule: White space is your best friend. If a paragraph is longer than two lines, it's too long.
The Markdown Format:
The Hook (Line 1): Bold, one sentence, under 10 words. It must "Stop the Scroll."
The Context (Lines 2-4): Short, punchy lines setting the scene.
The List (The "Meat"): Use 3-5 bullet points. This is where the brain scans for value.
The Pivot (The "Insight"): One sentence that flips the narrative.
The Solution (The Product): 2-3 lines about what you're building now.
The CTA (The Closer): One short line.

The Narrative Pivot (Converting Story to Sales)
The "Pivot" is the bridge. It's where you stop talking about you (the plastic stool) and start talking about the world (the merchant's pain). If you miss this, the post is just "look at me." If you hit it, the post is "this is why I can help you."
The Three "Cracked" Pivot Techniques:

1. The "Aha!" Realization (From Experience to Insight)
   You take a detail from your story and turn it into a universal business truth.
   The Move: "I realized that [Story Detail] is actually [Business Problem]."
   Example: "I realized that sitting on that stool wasn't my bottleneck—waiting 7 days for my payout was."
2. The "Evolution" (The Timeline Jump)
   You connect the past version of you to the current version of the product.
   The Move: "I'm still opening that laptop, but I'm solving a different problem now."
   Example: "Fast forward to 2026, and I've taken that same execution to the global payment layer."
3. The "I Wish I Had This" (The Founder's Origin)
   This is the most powerful way to pitch StellarTools without sounding like a salesman.
   The Move: "I'm building the tools I wish I had back then."
   Example: "Monetizing a product is still too hard for most developers. I built StellarTools to fix it."
   The "Cracked" Pivot Rule:
   Never spend more than 2 lines on the transition.
   The pivot should be a sharp "snap" in the narrative. It should make the reader think, "Of course he built that—it makes sense based on his story."

The Soft-Close and Algorithm Hacks
The final part of a "cracked" post isn't about begging for a sale; it's about inviting them into your orbit. If the post was good, they are already sold on you—the CTA (Call to Action) just tells them where to go next.

1. The "Soft-Close" CTA
   Justin Welsh rarely uses "Buy now." He uses "Join the journey" or "Check out the manual."
   The Move: State the destination and the value in one short line.
   Example: "Join the journey at StellarTools.dev." or "I'm building in public here: [Link]."
   Why it works: It feels like an invitation to a movement, not a sales pitch.
2. The "See More" Trap (Algorithm Hack #1)
   The LinkedIn algorithm rewards people who click "See More."
   The Hack: Keep your first 3 lines (the hook + the setup) so punchy that the user has to click to see the rest.
   Rule: If the "See More" button cuts off in the middle of your list, you win.
3. The "Native" Link Strategy (Algorithm Hack #2)
   External links in the body of a post can sometimes reduce reach, but "bare links" (text-only) are often safer than big preview cards.
   The Hack: Use a plain text URL at the very bottom.
   Pro-Tip: If you're worried about reach, post the link in the first comment and tell people: "I've put the link in the comments."
4. The "Raw over Polished" Rule (Algorithm Hack #3)
   As we discussed with your photo, authentic beats aesthetic.
   The Hack: Use phone-quality photos, screenshots of your code, or a messy desk.
   Why: People trust a founder who looks like they are actually working.

   Whitespace: One sentence per line.
   Bullet Points: 3-5 lines of "scannable" value.

   Soft-Close: Invite them to the journey, don't demand a purchase.
